Our Story

Born from a lifetime
of helping others.

WildWise didn't start with a grant or a strategic plan. It started with some kind people who thought they were helping a baby raccoon and a lack of information and resources that made the situation worse.

How It Started...

I grew up being the person people called when they found an animal in need.

I've always been the "animal" girl. I loved my cats and dogs more than anything growing up and have always been an animal enthusiast and advocate. One of my earliest memories was being dropped off by my dad at my mom's house with a box in hand that had a turtle in it. I told her me and dad rescued it. My mom asked me if I knew what the word "rescue" meant, to which I confidently replied, "Well yeah! It means turtle!". I used to start "businesses" as a kid, employing my little sister, to raise money for the WWF and our local animal shelter. Embarassingly, at one point, I even wrote a "rap" song and made her go door-to-door performing it for donations.

For as long as I can remember, animals have just sort of "found" me. The universe seems to likes to send them my way for some reason. I can't tell you how many lost or stray dogs I've invited into my car so I could help them find their way home. Because of my weird animal magnetism, over time, I became the person my friends called when they didn't know what to do. Well, if I was lucky they called. At one point, a friend knocked on my door and when I answered it, he handed me a FAWN and just said, "I didn't know what to do". Contrary to popular belief, I ALSO didn't know what to do. But, I love animals and helping is my whole identity, so I put the poor baby in a large tote to contain it and called a local wildlife rehab. I was told that they couldn't take fawns, but as long as it seemed okay and was healthy, I could put it back where it was found. Doing some research, I found out that fawns are often left alone for hours while their moms are off doing deer things. I was absolutely relieved to learn that I could likely reuinte this baby with it's mom. I asked the friend where his dogs found it, brought it back to that area, and soon had my first successful reunion. Returning dogs to their owners made me feel good, but returning a baby to its mom and wildlife to the wild made me feel like I was able to help in the most meaningful way. I Knew I Needed to Learn More

After talking with the rehabber and being able to successfully reunite that fawn, I knew I needed to learn more. I wanted to be able to help more animals and do it better. I started volunteering in some local Facebook groups for wildlife transportation. Essentially Uber for animals. It was when I joined this group that I started to see a heartbreaking pattern. Good, well-meaning people who found a baby, posting that they rescued it and had it in a box, were giving it food and water, and were looking to find somewhere to take it. It broke my heart hearing all these stories of animals who likely could have been reunited with their mom, but instead were found by someone who didn't know better, took them home, and in trying to care for them, were actually doing more harm than good. People were doing their best, but they just didn't have the right information. They didn't know what they didn't know. I started to see that there was a huge gap in education and resources for people who wanted to help but didn't know how. I also started to see that this wasn't just a problem for a few people here and there, but it was a widespread issue that was leading to a lot of unnecessary suffering for wildlife. That's when I knew I had to do something about it.

"My mom asked me what rescue meant. I said — it means turtle."

On growing up

Rescue, in our house, was as ordinary as dinner. A snapping turtle blocking traffic on the road in front of our house. My mom, standing at the window, watching me redirect cars. What are you doing? Rescue, Mom. It means turtle.

That's the foundation WildWise is built on. Not expertise for its own sake, but a deep belief that ordinary people, given the right information, can be extraordinary neighbors to wildlife. Most of the time, the most helpful thing a person can do is nothing. Knowing that — really knowing it — takes education.

WildWise is that education.

How it got personal

Noodle's story started with a family's love — and a serious mistake.

The crow who became our ambassador nearly died because good people didn't know what they didn't know. His story is the clearest argument for everything WildWise does.

A nestling crow, a well-meaning family.

A family found a crow nestling that had fallen from a nest. They did what anyone would do who cared and didn't know better: they brought it inside, named it, and started raising it. They fed it whatever seemed right. They held it constantly. They loved it.

What they didn't know — what nobody had ever told them — was that everything they were doing was teaching this crow that it was human. That it belonged indoors. That its family was people. That the wild was not where it lived.

By the time they called me, it was too late to reverse. The crow had imprinted. He couldn't be released — not because of any physical injury, but because of what that kind, caring family had accidentally taught him about the world.

That crow is Noodle. He's been with me ever since.

The lesson

What went wrong — and why it matters

The family made every one of the five most common mistakes. None of them were malicious. All of them were preventable.

Noodle is alive and thriving. But he can never be a wild crow. His wildness was taken from him — gently, lovingly, unknowingly.

The five mistakes

Five things people do that hurt the animals they're trying to help.

Every one of these comes from a place of caring. All five are preventable.

1 Mistake

Picking up healthy animals

Fawns curled in lawns, fledgling birds on the ground, rabbit kits in a nest — these animals are almost always exactly where they're supposed to be. The parents are nearby. "Alone" does not mean "orphaned."

What to do instead Observe from a distance for several hours. If the animal seems alert and uninjured, leave it alone.
2 Mistake

Feeding wildlife the wrong things

Bread, cow's milk, dog kibble, worms from the yard — none of these are appropriate for wildlife, and many are actively harmful. Feeding the wrong diet can cause irreversible metabolic damage in young animals.

What to do instead Do not feed. Contact a licensed rehabber immediately for guidance on diet and temporary care.
3 Mistake

Imprinting young birds on humans

Young birds (and some mammals) learn who they are by who raises them. Extended human handling, eye contact, and constant presence teaches them they are people. This cannot be reversed. Noodle is proof.

What to do instead Minimize handling, avoid eye contact, keep the animal in a dark, quiet container, and transfer to a rehabber as quickly as possible.
4 Mistake

Waiting too long to call for help

When an animal genuinely does need help, every hour matters. Stress, dehydration, and improper care compound quickly. The instinct to "try it at home first" often turns a recoverable animal into one that can't be saved.

What to do instead If the animal shows signs of injury or illness, call a licensed rehabber or Michigan DNR Wildlife Division immediately. Don't wait.
5 Mistake

Keeping wildlife as pets

It's illegal in Michigan to keep wild animals without a license. It's also deeply harmful to the animals — even when done with the best intentions. Wild animals raised in captivity lose their survival instincts and their wild identity.

What to do instead Contact a licensed wildlife rehabilitator. They have the permits, training, and species-appropriate environments to give animals their best chance at a wild life.

Our vision

Four ways WildWise is building
a better-informed Michigan.

We're starting in Ottawa County and growing from there.

01

Wildlife Ambassador Program

Noodle visits schools, libraries, and community events — bringing firsthand wildlife education to the people who need it most: kids growing up alongside Michigan wildlife.

02

Wildlife First Responder Training

A community training program that equips ordinary people with the knowledge to respond to wildlife encounters wisely. Coming soon — interest list open.

03

Transporter Network

We're building a volunteer driver network to bridge the gap between wildlife in need and the licensed rehabbers who can help them — faster, with less stress on the animal.

04

Partner Education

Working with parks, conservation orgs, nature centers, and vet clinics to make sure the people the public already trusts are giving accurate, helpful wildlife guidance.
