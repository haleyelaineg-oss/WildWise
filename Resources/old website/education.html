Wildlife Education

Know before you act.

Most wildlife "rescues" aren't rescues. Most animals "in need" are exactly where they're supposed to be. The most important thing you can know is when not to intervene.

The first question

Should I help this animal?

This is the most important question in wildlife rescue. The answer is almost always "no" — but here's how to know for sure.

✓ Yes — get help now

This animal needs immediate help

These are genuine emergencies. Do not wait. Contain the animal safely and contact a licensed wildlife rehabilitator or the Michigan DNR Wildlife Division right away.

  • ✓Bleeding, broken bones, or visible injuries
  • ✓Animal has been in a cat's or dog's mouth
  • ✓Bird or mammal that cannot move or stand
  • ✓Cold, wet, or lethargic newborn with no mother present
  • ✓Dead parent animal found nearby
Call rehabber immediately Handle minimally
✕ No — leave it alone

This animal is fine — walk away

These situations look like emergencies but aren't. The most helpful thing you can do is nothing. Intervention will cause more harm than it prevents.

  • ✕Fawn curled alone in grass or leaves
  • ✕Fledgling bird hopping on the ground (has short feathers)
  • ✕Baby rabbits in a nest in your yard
  • ✕Opossum playing dead
  • ✕Ducklings or goslings in a group without parent visible
Watch from distance Parent nearby
📞 Call first

You're not sure — call before you act

If you're uncertain, the best move is a phone call to a licensed rehabber before you intervene. They can walk you through what you're seeing in minutes and give you specific guidance.

The hardest lesson

Don't steal babies.

Every spring, thousands of healthy young animals are "rescued" from their perfectly normal situations by people who didn't know that alone doesn't mean orphaned. Some survive. Many don't. All of them would have been better off where they were.

Alone does not mean orphaned. Out of sight does not mean gone. Quiet does not mean dead.

A doe leaves her fawn hidden in grass for hours at a time while she forages. A mother rabbit visits her kits only at dusk and dawn to avoid drawing predators to the nest. A parent bird watches her fledgling hop across the yard from a branch twenty feet overhead. None of these animals are in distress. Their parent knows exactly where they are.

Common scenarios — and the truth

🦌
"There's a baby deer alone in my yard — it must be abandoned."
This is normal. Does hide their fawns and leave them for long periods. If the fawn is curled and quiet, it's waiting. Come back in 4–6 hours. The mother will return.
🐦
"A baby bird fell out of the nest — I have to save it."
If the bird has any feathers at all, it's a fledgling, not a nestling. Fledglings are supposed to be on the ground. They're learning to fly. Their parents are watching.
🐇
"I found a nest of baby rabbits — they have no mother."
Cottontail mothers visit their kits only twice a day — at dusk and dawn — to avoid attracting predators to the nest. An empty nest is a safe nest. Do not disturb it.
🐾
"This opossum is lying completely still — it must be dying."
"Playing possum" is a real, involuntary physiological response to fear. The animal is not in distress. Give it space and time. It will recover on its own.
The five mistakes

What almost killed Noodle — and how to avoid it.

These are the five mistakes the family who raised Noodle made. They're the same five mistakes made by well-meaning people every day. All five are preventable.

1 Mistake

They picked him up when they shouldn't have

Noodle's nest was nearby and intact. A nestling crow on the ground doesn't mean abandoned — it often means a clumsy fledgling or a short-term fall. The parents were watching. The family didn't know to look.

What to do Look for the nest. Try to place the bird back. Parent birds will NOT reject a baby touched by humans — that's a myth.
2 Mistake

They handled him constantly

He was held, petted, talked to, and carried around for weeks. Every moment of human contact was a moment of imprinting — teaching Noodle that humans were his flock, his family, his world.

What to do Minimize handling. Wear gloves or use a cloth barrier. No talking or eye contact. Keep in a dark, quiet box.
3 Mistake

They fed him the wrong things

Bread, crackers, cooked egg, whatever seemed right. Baby crows need specific protein sources, and improvised diets can cause permanent metabolic damage, especially in developing bones.

What to do Do not feed. Place in a dark container. Call a licensed rehabber for immediate guidance on diet.
4 Mistake

They waited weeks to call anyone

By the time they reached out, the imprinting window had long since closed. Noodle had been in their home for over a month. Early intervention would have allowed a rehabber to raise him with other crows, preserving his wild identity.

What to do Call the same day. Every hour with a human raises the risk. A rehabber can advise over the phone immediately.
5 Mistake

They didn't know any of this was a problem

The family did everything with love. Nobody ever told them about imprinting. Nobody told them the nest was still there. Nobody told them to call first. The information gap is the root cause of Noodle's non-releasable status.

That's why WildWise exists Education before the encounter. Not regret after.
Michigan species guide

Michigan wildlife you're likely to encounter.

Each species has its own patterns, its own seasons, and its own rules. Here's what to know about the animals most commonly "rescued" in Michigan — correctly and incorrectly.

🦌

White-tailed Deer

Fawns: May – June

Fawns alone in grass are almost never abandoned. The doe is nearby. Leave them.

🐇

Eastern Cottontail

Kits: March – September

Mother visits twice daily at dusk and dawn. An empty nest is a safe nest.

🦝

Raccoon

Kits: April – May

Rabies vector species. Never handle bare-handed. Kits crying for hours = call a rehabber.

🐦

Songbirds

Nestlings: April – August

Fledglings on the ground are learning to fly. Parents are watching. Leave them.

🦆

Mallard Duck

Ducklings: April – June

Ducklings separated from mom need help fast. Call immediately.

🪶

Canada Goose

Goslings: April – June

Goslings look vulnerable but parents are close. Don't separate them from the flock.

🐾

Virginia Opossum

Joeys: February – August

North America's only marsupial. Joeys smaller than 7" body length need rehabber care.
